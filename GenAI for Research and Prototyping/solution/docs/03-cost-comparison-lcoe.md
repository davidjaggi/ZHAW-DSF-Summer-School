# Research Note 3 — Cost Comparison (LCOE)

## Global benchmark LCOE (2024, IEA/IRENA weighted averages)

No Switzerland-specific LCOE series is publicly published at the same granularity as the global IEA/IRENA figures, so we use global weighted averages as the benchmark and adjust qualitatively for Swiss conditions below. All figures are **USD/kWh, new-build, unsubsidized**.

| Technology | Global weighted-average LCOE (2024) | Trend |
|---|---|---|
| Onshore wind | ~$0.034/kWh | Cheapest new-build source globally; falling |
| Solar PV (utility-scale) | ~$0.043/kWh | Roughly flat 2023→2024 after years of steep declines; still ~41% cheaper than the cheapest new fossil alternative |
| Hydropower (new build) | ~$0.057/kWh | Stable; site-dependent; existing large hydro is far cheaper (sunk cost, near-zero marginal cost) |
| Fossil (gas/coal) benchmark | higher than all of the above at 91% of newly commissioned utility-scale renewable projects | Reference point only — not a realistic option for Switzerland |
| Nuclear (new build) | not reliably captured by IEA/IRENA LCOE series (few new Western builds); international evidence (Flamanville FR, Hinkley Point C UK, Vogtle US) points to **$0.10–0.19/kWh** with large cost/time overruns | Historically rising, not falling, in Western markets |

## Applying this to Switzerland

- **Existing large hydro and the operating nuclear fleet** are effectively "sunk" assets with very low marginal generation costs (a few cents/kWh) — the *cheapest electricity Switzerland will ever have* is what it already has. The cost question that matters for the next decade is about **new investment**, not existing plants.
- **New-build solar** in the Swiss Plateau is cost-competitive with the global benchmark; **alpine solar** (Solarexpress-type projects) costs meaningfully more per kWh than lowland PV due to terrain, snow-load engineering, and short construction seasons, but delivers a disproportionate share of winter output, which has separate economic value (see Note 4) not captured by LCOE alone.
- **New-build wind** in the Alps/Jura is more expensive than the flat-terrain global average (transport, foundations, grid connection in mountainous terrain), but Switzerland's wind resource is genuinely small in scale, so the total cost of reaching even the modest 4.3 TWh/year long-term target is manageable in absolute (not per-kWh) terms.
- **New pumped-storage/hydro expansion** (e.g., Grimsel 4: CHF ~250 million for 150 MW, commissioning ~2031–2032) is capital-intensive per MW but buys **flexibility and storage**, not just energy — its economic value should be judged against balancing/import-avoidance costs, not plain LCOE.
- **New nuclear** would be built, if permitted, into one of the most expensive labor and regulatory environments in the world, with no domestic supply chain or recent construction experience (last unit connected: Leibstadt, 1984). International cost/schedule overruns (Flamanville: >€13bn, 12 years late; Hinkley Point C: >£40bn) are a realistic base case, not a worst case, for a first Swiss unit — implying **high cost and long lead time risk** that must be weighted heavily against LCOE tables.
- **Imports** are not "free": winter imports are priced at the marginal European price, which was extremely volatile in 2022–2023 and remains structurally higher in winter than Swiss domestic hydro cost — every TWh of import avoided by domestic investment has a hedging value beyond its own LCOE.

## Cost ranking for *new* Swiss investment (approximate, qualitative)

1. **Cheapest / lowest risk:** Plateau solar PV (rooftop + ground-mount), efficiency/demand-side measures
2. **Cheap, but land/grid constrained:** Additional lowland wind, small hydro upgrades
3. **Medium cost, high strategic value:** Alpine solar, pumped-storage expansion, grid reinforcement
4. **Higher cost, long lead time, high uncertainty:** New nuclear (contingent on the 2026+ referendum outcome)
5. **Ongoing hidden cost if under-invested:** Winter import dependency (price and availability risk)
