# Research Note 4 — Energy Security & Reliability

## The core problem: winter, not annual, supply

Switzerland is **not** short of electricity on an annual basis — it is short of *winter* electricity. In 8 of the last 10 winters, domestic production has not covered domestic demand:

- Average net winter imports over the last decade: **~4.5 TWh/winter (~15% of winter demand)**, peaking at 9.7 TWh in winter 2016/17.
- The 2024 Mantelerlass caps future net winter imports at **5 TWh**, effectively making "close the winter gap domestically" binding federal policy.
- ElCom (the Swiss electricity regulator) confirmed in May 2025 that supply is expected to hold through winter 2025 but that **strategic reserves remain necessary in the medium term**, citing import-capacity, renewable buildout pace, and demand growth as the main risk factors.

## Why each source behaves differently on reliability

- **Hydro (reservoir/storage):** Switzerland's single most valuable reliability asset — dispatchable on demand, and reservoirs can be timed to release in winter. Constrained by reservoir levels (which depend on prior snowmelt/rainfall) and by an already largely built-out resource — expansion is mostly about *efficiency and flexibility* (pumped storage like Grimsel 4), not new volume.
- **Nuclear:** High capacity factor, baseload, weather-independent — historically Switzerland's most reliable winter source. Existing fleet is aging (oldest units from the 1970s/69, Beznau I is one of the world's oldest operating reactors); reliability of the *existing* fleet depends on continued safety-driven maintenance/outages, which already cause year-to-year output swings.
- **Solar PV:** Structurally weak in winter (short days, low sun angle, potential snow cover) — lowland PV produces roughly 3–4x more in summer than winter. **Alpine/high-altitude PV is a partial fix**: above the winter fog layer, with snow reflection boosting output, alpine sites can generate roughly half their annual output in winter months, making them disproportionately valuable for security of supply even though they are a small fraction of total solar capacity.
- **Wind:** Complementary to solar (relatively stronger in winter, especially with Bise/föhn wind patterns), but Swiss wind capacity is still minuscule (well under 1 TWh/year today against a 4.3 TWh/year long-term target), so its current contribution to reliability is negligible and will stay modest even at target buildout.
- **Imports/interconnection:** Switzerland's grid position (bordering 5 countries, historically a European transit and balancing hub) is itself a reliability asset — but one increasingly in tension with the political goal of reducing import dependence, and exposed to European market conditions (e.g., French nuclear availability, which swung sharply in 2022).
- **Storage/flexibility (batteries, pumped hydro, demand response):** Not a generation source but the enabling technology that lets more solar/wind be integrated without compromising reliability; underinvestment here is a bigger near-term risk than underinvestment in any single generation technology.

## Stress-test findings

ETH Zurich's 2025 power-system stress tests and related analysis converge on a consistent message: Switzerland's winter security of supply is *manageable but not comfortable*, and depends on (a) faster renewables buildout, especially winter-favorable ones (alpine solar, wind, storage hydro), (b) continued reliable European market access, and (c) demand-side flexibility — rather than on any single "silver bullet" source.

## Implication for the recommendation

Energy security in the Swiss context is really a **portfolio/timing problem**: it rewards diversifying toward winter-productive sources (alpine PV, wind, flexible hydro/storage) and preserving import/export interconnection, more than it rewards maximizing any one technology's installed capacity.
