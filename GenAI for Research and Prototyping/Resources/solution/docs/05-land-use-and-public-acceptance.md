# Research Note 5 — Land Use & Public Acceptance

## Land use (generic, international literature — Switzerland has no equivalent official study at this granularity)

Land-use intensity per unit of *output* (not capacity) varies hugely by technology and site quality:

| Technology | Relative land footprint | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Nuclear | Lowest — cited as ~18–34x more land-efficient than hydro/solar per unit energy in US-based comparative studies | Small footprint, but siting is highly constrained by cooling water, seismic, and population-density rules |
| Fossil (for reference) | Low–medium | Not a realistic Swiss option |
| Wind | Low *direct* footprint, high *visual/spatial* footprint | Turbine pads are small, but each turbine needs wide spacing and access roads; land underneath often stays usable (farming/grazing) |
| Solar PV (ground-mount) | Medium–high | Fully occupies the land under panels; competes with agriculture in the Plateau, or with alpine/pastoral land at altitude |
| Hydropower (reservoirs) | Highest per unit of *new* output, but Switzerland's reservoirs are historic, multi-purpose (flood control, tourism, irrigation) infrastructure already built | Almost no new large reservoir sites remain; expansion is via efficiency (Grimsel 4-type projects), not new land take |

**Swiss-specific nuance:** Switzerland's land constraint is less about absolute hectares (a small, densely populated country) and more about **which** land: the Plateau is agriculturally and residentially valuable and politically sensitive for ground-mount solar; the Alps are visually/environmentally sensitive (landscape protection, tourism economy) but offer the best winter solar and most available open space. This tension **is** the Swiss solar siting debate, not a side issue.

## Public acceptance

- **Nuclear:** Historically decisive — the 2017 referendum enshrined the phase-out policy (no new plants) by popular vote. That said, the 2024–2026 "Stop the Blackout" campaign shows the political consensus has weakened amid winter-security and cost-of-living concerns; the outcome of the pending referendum is genuinely uncertain. Public opinion is visibly split, not settled either way.
- **Alpine solar:** Mixed but more favorable than often assumed — local communities have approved **roughly half of all planned Solarexpress-type projects** put to a cantonal/communal vote (e.g., Valais votes), suggesting acceptance is achievable with local buy-in and revenue sharing, but is not automatic and can be lost (some cantons have rejected accelerated planning procedures for solar).
- **Wind:** The **most locally contested** renewable technology in Switzerland today. Two dedicated federal popular initiatives were validated in September 2025 specifically to constrain wind development (forest protection framing, and direct-democracy/cantonal-veto framing). Opposition is described in Swiss energy-sector commentary as "not massive, but well-organised and well-funded" — a small but effective blocking coalition rather than broad public rejection.
- **Hydropower:** Generally the most broadly accepted source (long history, "Swiss identity" association, multi-purpose reservoirs), but new projects still face environmental/fish-migration and landscape-protection objections, and cantonal water-rights politics are a factor in expansion speed.
- **Cross-cutting factor — direct democracy itself:** Switzerland's three-tier referendum system (federal/cantonal/communal) is a genuine acceptance *mechanism*, not just an obstacle — projects that clear local votes tend to have durable local legitimacy — but it structurally slows and adds uncertainty to deployment timelines for every technology, which is why the 2025/2026 legal reforms focus on streamlining procedure (single cantonal approval process) rather than removing votes.

## Implication for the recommendation

Public acceptance risk is now the **single largest source of implementation-time uncertainty** in the Swiss mix — larger than technology or cost risk for solar and wind, and larger than the engineering timeline for nuclear (where the *political* question of whether it's even legal is more binding than the ~10–15 year build time itself). Any credible 10-year plan must budget for contested local votes as a normal part of delivery, and should prioritize technologies/siting strategies with demonstrated acceptance (alpine PV with local revenue-sharing, hydro efficiency upgrades) over those with organized, active opposition (new large wind farms, new nuclear pending the referendum).
