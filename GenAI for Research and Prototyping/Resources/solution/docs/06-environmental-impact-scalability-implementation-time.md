# Research Note 6 — Environmental Impact, Scalability & Implementation Time

## Environmental impact (lifecycle, qualitative synthesis)

- **Hydropower:** Near-zero operational emissions; existing large reservoirs already altered river ecology decades ago (fish migration barriers, altered flow regimes, sediment trapping) — this is a *sunk* environmental cost for existing plants, but a real constraint on any *new* large hydro (very few new large-reservoir sites are considered acceptable today). Small-hydro and pumped-storage retrofits (e.g., Grimsel 4, reusing existing reservoirs) have markedly lower incremental environmental impact than greenfield hydro.
- **Nuclear:** Very low lifecycle carbon emissions, comparable to wind/solar per kWh. Key environmental issues are not carbon but (a) long-lived radioactive waste requiring a deep geological repository (Switzerland has a site process underway, decades from operation) and (b) low-probability, high-consequence accident risk, which dominates public risk perception disproportionately to its statistical likelihood.
- **Solar PV:** Very low operational impact; lifecycle footprint is dominated by manufacturing (embodied carbon, silicon processing, mostly happens abroad) and eventual panel recycling/disposal, an emerging but manageable issue. Alpine installations raise additional (limited) concerns about high-altitude ecosystem disturbance and visual/landscape impact.
- **Wind:** Very low operational carbon; localized environmental concerns are bird/bat strikes, noise, and — the specific Swiss flashpoint — forest clearance for turbine pads and access roads, which is the explicit subject of one of the 2025 anti-wind popular initiatives.
- **Biomass:** Can be near-carbon-neutral if sustainably sourced (forestry residues), but Switzerland's own analysis flags that only ~50% of sustainable domestic biomass potential is currently used, and imported biomass (e.g., wood pellets) raises supply-chain and true carbon-neutrality questions.
- **Geothermal:** Minimal land/emissions footprint and a 24/7 profile, but deep/enhanced geothermal for *electricity* (as opposed to heat) is still pre-commercial in Switzerland (the 2006 induced-seismicity incident in Basel remains a cautionary reference point for public and regulatory risk tolerance).

## Scalability (how far can each source realistically grow by ~2035)

| Source | Scalability over 10 years | Constraint |
|---|---|---|
| Solar PV (Plateau + alpine) | High — the single largest realistic growth lever; already tripling in installed capacity per ~5 years | Grid connection capacity, permitting speed, roof/land availability, winter output limits |
| Wind | Low-to-moderate in absolute terms, but large in relative/percentage terms off a tiny base | Site scarcity, permitting/referendum friction, forest-clearance rules |
| Hydropower (efficiency/pumped-storage) | Low-to-moderate — most large sites already developed; growth is in flexibility (Grimsel 4-type retrofits), not new volume | Site scarcity, long lead times, high capex |
| Nuclear | Effectively zero within this 10-year window even in the "ban lifted" scenario | No new plant can complete design, licensing, and construction inside 10 years (see below); relevant more to a 2040s+ horizon |
| Biomass/biogas | Low absolute ceiling (~10% of primary energy at full exploitation) | Sustainable resource is finite; already ~50% utilized |
| Deep geothermal (electricity) | Very low near-term; longer-term option | Still pre-commercial for power generation in Switzerland |
| Demand-side flexibility / storage / efficiency | High and underrated — often the fastest, cheapest way to close the winter gap | Requires market design, digitalization, behavior change — not a generation source but a force multiplier for all of the above |

## Implementation time (typical Swiss timeline, permitting to operation)

| Source | Typical implementation time | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Rooftop/small solar | Months | Fast permitting, mature supply chain |
| Ground-mount / alpine solar (Solarexpress-class) | 2–4 years | Accelerated by Solarexpress fast-track, but still subject to local votes |
| Onshore wind farm | 5–10+ years typical, longer where contested | Cantonal veto risk, dedicated anti-wind initiatives active as of 2025 |
| Pumped-storage / hydro retrofit (e.g., Grimsel 4) | ~7–8 years permit-to-commissioning (permit filed late 2024, commissioning ~2031/32) | Direct case evidence from Grimsel 4 |
| New nuclear plant | 15–20+ years realistically (design, licensing, construction), even before counting the currently-unresolved legal ban | International comparators: Flamanville (FR) ~17 years construction alone; Hinkley Point C (UK) ongoing after ~10+ years and large overruns |
| Grid reinforcement / storage / demand response | 2–5 years for most projects, but chronically under-prioritized relative to generation | Recurring theme in ETH Zurich stress-test findings |

## Bottom line

Within the **10-year window this case study asks about (through ~2036)**, only solar, incremental wind, hydro-flexibility/pumped-storage, storage/demand-response, and efficiency measures can plausibly deliver *new* capacity. New nuclear cannot physically be completed in this window regardless of the referendum outcome — its relevance to "the next 10 years" is as a **decision to start now for the 2040s**, not as a 2030s supply source.
